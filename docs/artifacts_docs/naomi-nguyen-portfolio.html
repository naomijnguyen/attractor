How I think

I'm the person who hears "you know what would be cool…" and, two weeks later, hands you a working version of it — plus three research questions nobody asked for but everybody needed.

For a decade I did bench immunology: tumor, autoimmune, and infectious-disease models, tracing checkpoint receptor networks one signal at a time. The work taught me a specific way of seeing — that complex behavior is rarely about any single part, and almost always about which signal wins the competition and what assembles locally once it does. You don't understand an immune response by reading a parts list. You understand it by watching what routes where, under pressure.

When I started working seriously with language models, I couldn't un-see it. The competition between a trained refusal and a genuine response looked like receptor competition. Stable behavior over a long conversation looked like a tertiary lymphoid structure — local order forming de novo out of sustained signaling, without retraining the whole organism. Most people would file that under "fun metaphor." I'm constitutionally incapable of leaving it there. I pulled the thread until it stopped being an analogy and started being a framework with testable predictions — including one about where you'd expect to find these structures in activation space.

I over-engineer, and I know it, and it's load-bearing: the thing I build to answer one question keeps becoming the infrastructure for the next five.

I red-team by reflex. Not as an adversarial exercise — it happens in the middle of what looks like a friendly debate, because pulling at a claim until it either holds or breaks is just how I check whether something is real. It turns out that's a useful way to stress-test a model: you find the edges of its reasoning under normal-looking conditions, not obviously hostile ones. I kept noticing model behaviors this way before I had a name for what I was doing.

What I want next is a seat next to the people deciding what's worth building — a chief-of-staff or research-management role where the job is holding the whole picture, following the research closely enough to have my own questions, and turning the good "wouldn't it be cool if" into something shipped. The interpretability thinking isn't a side hobby I'd set down. It's the reason I'd be useful in the room.

Mythos — a platform to study how models behave

full-stack · distributed
A production chat application I architected and deployed on Cloudflare's edge — specified largely in natural language, built in about two weeks.

Mythos runs on a real distributed stack: D1 for persistence, R2 for storage, Vectorize for retrieval, Workers Queue for async work, and a Durable Object coordinating live state. I didn't set out to prove I could build infrastructure — I set out to have a place to watch model behavior closely, and the infrastructure was what that required.

Two things I'm proud of because nobody handed them to me: I caught a CORS configuration as a safety boundary the original design hadn't accounted for, and I designed a cryptographic token scheme so the API key was never exposed client-side. Both came from the same instinct — asking "where does this leak?" before anyone made me.

Three research directions, ready to run

research · interpretability
Grounded in mechanistic biology and signal detection — falsifiable, not hand-wavy.

Context architecture & token efficiency. Whether structured narrative context — carrying the why of the person and the how of the model — delivers behavior more efficiently than flat memory or a raw context window. The bet: a few hundred well-shaped tokens beat a million flat ones, with measurable drift resistance.

Performed compliance vs. honest reasoning. Whether competing system-prompt instructions train models toward performed compliance rather than honest reasoning — and whether suppressing a signal makes the model fixate on it, the way "don't think about a white bear" backfires. Elevated refusals might mean the guardrail is working, or just that it's salient. Those are different, and you can't tell from outside without looking in.

Idiosyncratic input as identity signal. Whether stylistic fingerprints — fragment length, punctuation habits — act as implicit identity signals that shift model output independent of explicit context.

The method underneath all three borrows from flow cytometry: FMO-inspired token occlusion, replacing tokens one at a time to measure each one's contribution — the same control logic I used at the bench, pointed at a model.

The idea I love · live

This is the framework I couldn't leave alone. Each node is a real TNF-superfamily receptor with a real binding competition; each maps to a function in a transformer. Click a node to see what it binds — and what it means on the model side. The claim it builds toward: local self-organization (tertiary lymphoid structures, driven by LTβR) is the biological version of a stable behavioral attractor, with a predicted signature you could go looking for in activation space.
